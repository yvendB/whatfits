01

The problem

It's late, and calories are still open. You walk through your cupboard in your head, type 80 g of rice into the tracker, correct it to 120, add corn, recalculate, drop the chicken again. It takes far too long — and you routinely forget food you actually have.

Above all, what comes out is unusable in real life. Nobody opens a tin for 37 grams of sweetcorn.

02

The idea

You enter your remaining budget — or it arrives automatically — and the app proposes combinations from your own food that hit that budget. What separates this from a plain calculator is a single concept:

The portion grid

Every food gets a permitted step size. Corn may only appear as whole or half tins, peanuts in 5-gram steps, a chicken breast only as one piece. The suggestion is therefore not rounded into shape afterwards — the grid is part of the search space, not cosmetics applied at the end.

Both ways of capturing the budget stay equally supported: typed in by hand for everyone who deliberately does not want their apps connected, and read automatically for everyone else. The manual path must never become second class.

04

The solver

The core of the product. Input is the remaining budget with tolerances, the pantry with its grids, the meal type and the user's preferences; output is several combinations ranked by how well they fit.

Because every product may only contribute amounts from its own grid, the search space is discrete and small. Each candidate is ranked by a weighted score:

// lower is better
score = w_kcal  · deviation(kcal)        // asymmetric: over ≠ under
      + w_macro · deviation(P, C, F)
      + w_pack  · partial_pack_penalty   // odd leftovers cost, whole packs are free
      − w_open  · use_up_bonus           // clearing an open pack pays off
      + w_rep   · repetition_penalty     // not the same thing five days running
      + w_n     · component_count        // three ingredients beat six

Pre-filter by meal and category, then depth-first search with branch and bound over at most three to five components. With 20 to 40 products in the pantry this resolves in milliseconds on the device — no server required, and the core feature works entirely offline.

Architecture

The solver belongs in a standalone, platform-independent module covered by unit tests, kept separate from the UI framework. It is the app's one genuine differentiator, and at the same time the part that is easiest to test — and to show in a portfolio.

What the user controls

  • Target accuracy — how precisely does the budget have to be hit?
  • May I go over? Overshoot allowed yes/no, and if yes, by how much.
  • Partial-pack rules per product or category — tins whole or half only, nuts to the gram.
  • Maximum number of components per suggestion.

These four settings are the weights of the score function, translated into plain language.

05

Product data

Users need to be able to add their own products — otherwise the one store brand they actually buy is always missing. Two stages keep the database from rotting.

Automatic plausibility check

  • Calculated calories: kcal ≈ 4·carbs + 4·protein + 9·fat. If the entered value deviates by more than roughly 20 %, warn.
  • Hard bounds: macros sum to at most 100 g per 100 g, no single value above that, and at most ~900 kcal per 100 g — which would be pure fat.
  • Order of magnitude against the category: 500 g of rice is ~600 kcal, not 6,000. This catches decimal-place typos reliably.

Anything suspicious, or used especially often, additionally goes into community review.

Cold start — to resolve

An empty product database makes the app unusable. The obvious base layer is Open Food Facts — open, barcode-indexed, millions of products.

Its licence (ODbL) does, however, require attribution and imposes conditions on derived databases. That has to be settled before commercial use, not after.

06

Context & community

Meals

Split into breakfast, lunch, dinner and snack. This matters to the solver, because different food makes sense per meal — the suggestion sets for different meals should overlap as little as possible.

Suggestions from history

From the usage data of recent weeks, the app learns which products fit which budget situations — and reminds you of food you keep forgetting despite having it in the cupboard. That is precisely the mistake people make doing this in their head.

Community dishes

Users publish finished combinations as dishes, which are then offered to others as ready-made suggestions. Community points on the profile are the incentive to contribute data — and the mechanism by which suggestion quality grows beyond any single user's pantry.

07

Business model

  • The app is free by default, funded by ads.
  • A Pro subscription removes them. Price range in line with Yazio — deliberately cheap; this is not a premium product.

09

Tech & risk

Tracker integration

A direct Yazio integration is unrealistic: there is no public API, and it would require a partnership. The practical route is the platform's own health store — Apple Health, and Health Connect on Android. Trackers write their nutrition data there; WhatToEat reads today's consumed calories and macros and derives the remainder itself. This works without any cooperation from Yazio — and with every other tracker at the same time.

To verify: whether Yazio actually writes nutrition data to Apple Health in the version in use, and at what granularity.

Stack

The solver as a standalone, tested module. Above it a cross-platform UI — Flutter or React Native — so that phase 5 does not mean building everything twice, with native bridges for HealthKit and Health Connect. A backend is only needed from phase 2/3 onwards; for a solo developer, a backend-as-a-service beats self-hosted infrastructure, and relational Postgres fits product data well. Pantry and solver stay offline-first.

Scope — and the one real risk

WhatToEat is not a calorie tracker and does not aim to become one. It is a companion for the last decision of the day. That keeps the product small and sharp, but it also means a permanent dependency on the app the user already runs. That is the central strategic risk — it should be carried deliberately, not defined away.
